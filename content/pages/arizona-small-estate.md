## What Is an Arizona Small Estate Affidavit?

An Arizona Small Estate Affidavit is a sworn legal document that allows heirs to claim a deceased person's assets — bank accounts, vehicles, personal property, or real estate — without opening a probate case in court. It is authorized under ARS §14-3971 and can represent a significant savings in both time and cost compared to formal probate.

The affidavit works by the heir(s) presenting a signed, notarized document to the asset holder (a bank, DMV, or property registrar) that states they are legally entitled to the property. The asset holder is then legally permitted — and generally obligated — to release the property to the heir.

## The 2026 Law Change: What HB2116 Actually Changed

Before HB2116, Arizona's small estate thresholds were substantially lower, and many people with mid-sized estates had no choice but to go through probate. The 2026 amendment represents the most significant update to Arizona small estate law in years.

**New thresholds effective March 2026 (HB2116):**

| Asset Type | New Limit (March 2026) | Prior Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Personal property | $200,000 | Lower — varies by prior year threshold |
| Real property | $300,000 | Lower — varies by prior year threshold |

Personal property includes bank accounts, investment accounts, vehicles, household goods, and most assets that are not real estate. Real property means land and structures — homes, rental properties, commercial real estate.

**Why this matters:** A family home worth $280,000 that previously would have required a full probate proceeding can now pass through a small estate affidavit under the new real property threshold. That potentially saves thousands of dollars in probate attorney fees and months of delay.

## Who Qualifies to Use an Arizona Small Estate Affidavit?

To use the Arizona Small Estate Affidavit process under ARS §14-3971, you must meet all of the following conditions:

**Eligibility checklist:**

- [ ] The deceased person's assets (personal property OR real property, depending on what you're claiming) fall within the applicable threshold
- [ ] At least 30 days have passed since the date of death
- [ ] No probate proceeding is currently pending or has been commenced for the estate
- [ ] You are an heir under Arizona intestate succession law OR the successor to the deceased's interest in the property

The 30-day waiting period is firm. Unlike some states where the waiting period can be waived in certain circumstances, Arizona requires you to wait the full 30 days before presenting the affidavit.

## Personal Property vs. Real Property: Two Different Processes

One of the most confusing aspects of Arizona's small estate law is that personal property and real property follow slightly different procedures.

### Personal Property (Bank Accounts, Vehicles, Investments)

For personal property under $200,000, you present the affidavit directly to the holder of the property — the bank, credit union, DMV, or investment firm. The holder is authorized to transfer the property to you without a court order.

Required elements:
- Statement that 30 days have elapsed since date of death
- Statement that no probate is pending
- Description of the property you are claiming
- Your relationship to the deceased and basis for your claim
- The total value of personal property in the estate (to confirm it is under $200,000)
- Notarized signature

### Real Property (Real Estate)

For real property under $300,000, the affidavit must be **recorded with the county recorder** in the county where the property is located — not just presented to an institution. This creates a public record establishing the transfer.

Key differences for real property:
- Must be recorded, not just presented
- Property value is assessed as the fair market value, not the assessed value
- The affidavit must include the legal description of the property (from the deed, not just the address)
- Requires notarization

## Step-by-Step: Filing an Arizona Small Estate Affidavit

### Step 1: Confirm the Waiting Period

Check that at least 30 days have passed since the date of death. The affidavit will be rejected or legally ineffective if presented before the waiting period expires. The date of death is established by the death certificate.

### Step 2: Determine the Asset Type and Value

Add up the total value of either the personal property or real property you are claiming. This determines which threshold applies ($200,000 for personal property, $300,000 for real property) and which form to use.

If an estate has both personal and real property, each category is evaluated separately against its own threshold.

### Step 3: Obtain the Correct Form

Use the form appropriate for your county. Maricopa County uses Superior Court probate division forms. Pima and Yavapai have their own. For other counties, Arizona Judicial Branch (azcourts.gov) publishes general forms.

**smallestateform.com generates Arizona-specific forms automatically matched to your county** based on the information you provide.

### Step 4: Complete and Notarize

Fill out the form with accurate information about the deceased, the estate, and your relationship to the deceased. Then sign in front of a notary public.

### Step 5: Present or Record

- **Personal property:** Present the notarized affidavit directly to the asset holder (bank, DMV, brokerage) along with the death certificate and your ID.
- **Real property:** Record the notarized affidavit with the county recorder in the county where the property is located.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Who qualifies to use an Arizona Small Estate Affidavit?**
Any heir under Arizona intestate succession law, or any person who is the legal successor to the deceased's interest in the property. There must be no pending probate proceeding and at least 30 days must have passed since death.

**What assets are covered by the $200,000 personal property threshold?**
Bank accounts, savings accounts, CDs, investment accounts, vehicles, household goods, jewelry, and other personal assets. Life insurance proceeds and retirement accounts with named beneficiaries generally pass outside of the estate and do not count.

**How does the 30-day waiting period work exactly?**
You must wait 30 full days from the date listed on the death certificate. If the date of death was July 1, the earliest you can present the affidavit is July 31. Some institutions may ask for proof of the date of death before accepting the affidavit.

**What if the estate is over $200,000 in personal property?**
If the personal property exceeds $200,000 (or real property exceeds $300,000 as of March 2026), formal probate is generally required. Consult an Arizona probate attorney.

**Do you need an attorney to file an Arizona Small Estate Affidavit?**
No. The process is designed to be done without an attorney. However, if there are disputes among heirs, debts that complicate the estate, or the property exceeds the thresholds, an attorney's guidance is advisable.

**Can a Small Estate Affidavit be used for real property in Arizona?**
Yes — and this is one of Arizona's more permissive features. Under ARS §14-3971 as amended by HB2116, real property valued under $300,000 can be transferred by affidavit. The affidavit must be recorded with the county recorder, not just presented to an institution.

## When This Document May Not Help You

This document may not help you if:

**The estate exceeds the applicable threshold (gross value, not equity).** Arizona measures the threshold against the fair market value of the asset, not equity. A home worth $320,000 with a $200,000 mortgage does not qualify under the $300,000 real property limit — gross value ($320,000) exceeds the threshold regardless of what is owed on it.

**The mandatory 30-day waiting period has not passed.** Arizona's 30-day requirement is firm and cannot be waived. Presenting an affidavit before 30 days have elapsed makes the document legally defective, and a bank or county recorder who catches this may refuse it entirely.

**A probate proceeding is already open for this estate.** If anyone has filed a probate petition in any Arizona Superior Court for this estate, the Small Estate Affidavit process is unavailable. Check the Superior Court's online case records before proceeding.

**The property has complex title issues.** Mechanic's liens, tax liens, boundary disputes, or title problems with the existing deed may require court-supervised resolution that the affidavit process cannot provide. A title company review is advisable before filing.

## What Banks Commonly Reject — and What to Do About It

Arizona's ARS §14-3971 gives banks clear legal authority to honor the affidavit, but individual bank employees don't always know the law. Here are the most common rejection scenarios and how to address them:

**Affidavit presented before 30 days have passed.** Banks that check the date of death against the date of presentation may refuse an early submission. The 30-day period is calculated from the date listed on the death certificate — wait the full period.

**Missing or illegible notary seal.** Arizona banks expect a stamped or embossed notary seal alongside the notary's signature. A handwritten-only acknowledgment without a seal raises questions about validity.

**Death certificate not brought along.** Always present a certified death certificate with the affidavit. Banks generally require it as supporting documentation, even if the statute does not mandate it in every case.

**Account balance over the bank's internal approval threshold.** Some national banks have internal policies requiring branch manager sign-off for accounts above $5,000–$25,000. This is the bank's own policy, not a legal requirement. Escalate to a manager and present the bank instruction letter.

**Probate appears to have been opened.** If a probate proceeding was filed and is visible in court records, the bank may hesitate. Confirm no probate is pending before presenting the affidavit.

**Employee unfamiliarity with ARS §14-3971.** The most common cause of delay. The **bank instruction letter** included with every smallestateform.com document cites the statute directly, explains the bank's legal authority, and has been designed specifically for this situation. Ask to speak with the branch manager.

## Mistakes People Make When Filing in Arizona

**Using gross value to disqualify themselves.** Many families look at a home worth $280,000 with a $180,000 mortgage and assume the estate is "worth $100,000 net" — which is far under the threshold. But they also incorrectly assume that means they clearly qualify. The calculation is correct here (gross $280,000 is under $300,000), but families sometimes make the opposite error: seeing a $310,000 gross value and assuming they qualify because their equity is only $130,000. Gross value controls.

**Submitting the affidavit without the death certificate.** The death certificate is essential documentation at every step — both for the county recorder and for banks. Never submit without it.

**Using an outdated form showing pre-HB2116 thresholds.** A form that lists the old, lower limits is not just wrong — it may cause the bank or title company to question the validity of the entire document. Use only forms that reflect the March 2026 HB2116 thresholds.

**Not distinguishing between personal and real property procedures.** Personal property goes to the asset holder directly; real property must be recorded with the county recorder. Presenting a real property affidavit to a bank (without recording it first) is a common procedural error that causes unnecessary delays.

**Forgetting to count all personal property for the threshold calculation.** The $200,000 personal property limit applies to the total personal property in the estate, not just the account you are trying to access. If the estate has multiple accounts totaling more than $200,000, the Small Estate Affidavit process may not be available.

This waiting period is firm and cannot be waived under any circumstances. Calculate 30 full calendar days from the date on the death certificate before presenting the document.

**Does the Arizona Small Estate Affidavit work for a house?** Yes — and this is one of Arizona's most permissive features. Under ARS §14-3971 as amended by HB2116 in March 2026, real property valued under $300,000 (gross fair market value) can be transferred by affidavit. The affidavit must be recorded with the county recorder, not just presented to an institution. Personal property and real property are evaluated separately against their own thresholds.

**How does Arizona's Small Estate Affidavit compare to formal probate?** The affidavit avoids Arizona Superior Court entirely. Total cost is $30–$50 in county recorder fees versus $3,000–$15,000+ for formal probate attorney fees. Timeline is approximately five to seven weeks (including the 30-day wait) versus six to eighteen months for probate. The tradeoff: probate provides stronger creditor protection and is required when the estate exceeds the thresholds or heirs are in dispute.
