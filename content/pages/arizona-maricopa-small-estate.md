## Maricopa County vs. the Rest of Arizona: What's Different

Arizona's statewide Small Estate Affidavit law (ARS §14-3971) applies in Maricopa County — but the **forms and recording procedures are county-specific**. What works in Pima or Yavapai County may not be the right form for Maricopa.

For most personal property (bank accounts, vehicles, household goods): the statewide process applies — present the notarized affidavit to the asset holder.

For **real property in Maricopa County**: you must record the affidavit with the **Maricopa County Recorder**, and you should use forms consistent with Maricopa Superior Court probate division requirements.

## Step-by-Step: Maricopa County Real Property Process

### Step 1: Confirm the 30-Day Waiting Period

Check the date of death on the death certificate. You cannot record the affidavit until at least 30 days after that date.

### Step 2: Verify the Property Value and Threshold

Determine the current fair market value of the real property. If it exceeds $300,000, the Small Estate Affidavit process is not available — formal probate or another transfer mechanism will be needed.

### Step 3: Use the Correct Form

Maricopa County recommends using forms consistent with the Maricopa Superior Court's probate division. These are available:
- Through the Maricopa County Superior Court Self-Service Center
- From a document generator like smallestateform.com (which auto-selects Maricopa County forms)

### Step 4: Include Required Elements

The affidavit must contain:
- [ ] Deceased's full name and date of death
- [ ] Statement that 30 days have elapsed
- [ ] Statement that no probate proceeding is pending
- [ ] Description of the real property (legal description, not just address)
- [ ] Assessed value or fair market value statement
- [ ] Affiant's relationship to the deceased and basis for the claim
- [ ] Names of all known heirs
- [ ] Notarized signature

### Step 5: Notarize

Sign the affidavit in front of a notary public. The Maricopa County Recorder will not accept an unnotarized affidavit.

### Step 6: Record at the Maricopa County Recorder

Bring or mail the original notarized affidavit to 111 S 3rd Ave, Phoenix, AZ 85003. Pay the $30 first-page fee plus $3 per additional page.

### Step 7: Obtain Certified Copies

After recording, request certified copies. Banks and title companies require certified copies — not photocopies — when accepting the recorded affidavit as proof of transfer.

## Frequently Asked Questions: Maricopa County

**Do I file the Maricopa County Small Estate Affidavit at the Superior Court or the Recorder?**
For real property, you record with the **Maricopa County Recorder** (111 S 3rd Ave, Phoenix). You do not file a petition with the Superior Court — the affidavit process is designed to bypass the court entirely. The Recorder records the transfer; the Court is not involved unless there is a dispute.

**What if the estate has both personal property and real property in Maricopa County?**
Each is handled separately. Personal property (bank accounts, vehicles) — present the affidavit to the institution. Real property — record with the Maricopa County Recorder. Each must independently fall within its respective threshold ($200,000 personal, $300,000 real).

**Does the Maricopa Small Estate Affidavit expire?**
There is no statutory expiration, but recording it promptly is advisable. Institutions and title companies grow more skeptical when an unrecorded or recently-recorded affidavit relates to an older death.

**Can I file by mail with the Maricopa County Recorder?**
Yes. Mail the original notarized affidavit with payment and a self-addressed stamped envelope to 111 S 3rd Ave, Phoenix, AZ 85003. Turnaround is typically 1–3 business days after receipt.

**What if someone disputes my claim to the property?**
A Small Estate Affidavit does not extinguish other heirs' legal claims. If there is a legitimate dispute among heirs, the affidavit process should be approached cautiously — a disputed estate is generally better handled through formal probate where a court can adjudicate the competing claims.

## When This Document May Not Help You

This document may not help you if:

**The gross fair market value of real property exceeds $300,000.** Maricopa County follows the statewide HB2116 threshold. The calculation uses gross value — not equity. A home worth $320,000 with a $220,000 mortgage does not qualify; the gross value ($320,000) is over the limit regardless of what is owed.

**Less than 30 days have passed since the date of death.** Arizona's waiting period is firm. Recording an affidavit or presenting it to a bank before 30 days have elapsed makes the document legally defective. The Maricopa County Recorder may accept the filing without catching this, but a title company conducting a future title search will.

**A probate proceeding has been filed in Maricopa Superior Court.** Check the court's online case search before proceeding. If anyone has opened a probate case for this estate, the affidavit route is closed until that proceeding closes.

**The property has title complications.** Existing liens, mechanic's liens, HOA disputes, or encumbrances on title do not disappear when the affidavit is recorded. A title company reviewing the property for a future sale will identify these separately. For complex title situations, a probate attorney's review is advisable.

## What the Maricopa County Recorder Returns — and What to Do

Most Maricopa County Recorder rejections are preventable. Common reasons:

**Missing legal property description.** A street address alone will not suffice. The affidavit must include the legal description as it appears in the existing deed — lot, block, subdivision, or metes-and-bounds. Pull it directly from the grant deed or the Maricopa County Assessor's online records.

**Insufficient fee.** The Recorder charges $30 for the first page plus $3 per additional page. An underpayment causes the submission to be returned without recording. Count all pages of the affidavit carefully before submitting.

**Unnotarized document.** The Maricopa County Recorder will not record an affidavit that does not bear a notary's seal and signature. If your document is returned for this reason, it must be entirely re-executed before a notary — not just have a notary acknowledgment added afterward.

**Missing ARS §14-3971 eligibility statements.** The affidavit must explicitly state: (1) that 30 days have elapsed, (2) that no probate proceeding is pending, and (3) the affiant's relationship to the deceased and basis for their claim. An affidavit that omits these statutory elements may be returned or may later be challenged.

## Mistakes People Make With Maricopa County Filings

**Filing at the Maricopa Superior Court instead of the Recorder.** The Small Estate Affidavit for real property in Maricopa County is recorded with the **Maricopa County Recorder** (111 S 3rd Ave, Phoenix) — not with the Superior Court. The Superior Court is involved only in formal probate. Many people go to the wrong office.

**Presenting a real property affidavit to a bank without recording it first.** For real property, the affidavit must be recorded with the Recorder before it is presented anywhere else. Recording creates the public record that makes it effective against third parties. Skipping the recording step and going straight to a bank is a procedural error.

**Using a photocopy instead of a certified copy.** After recording, request certified copies from the Recorder. Banks and title companies require certified copies — not photocopies, not PDFs — to accept the affidavit as proof of transfer.

**Not budgeting for Maricopa County's fees.** The $30-first-page plus $3-per-additional-page fee structure means a 4-page affidavit costs $39 to record. Come with exact payment or a check payable to "Maricopa County Recorder." Confirm current payment methods before filing, as card acceptance varies.
