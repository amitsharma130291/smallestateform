## What Changed in August 2025 — and Why It Matters

The Illinois Probate Act's small estate provision (755 ILCS 5/25-1) was amended in August 2025, raising the statewide threshold for the Small Estate Affidavit and updating certain procedural requirements.

**The practical effect:** Many families who previously had no choice but to open a formal probate estate — a process that can cost $3,000–$15,000+ in attorney fees and take 9–18 months — can now use a small estate affidavit instead. The amendment brought Illinois in line with the reality that even modest estates often exceed older, lower thresholds.

**If a guide, form, or tool you've found reflects the old law:** Stop and use current materials. An affidavit filed using an outdated form or citing outdated threshold figures may be rejected by banks and other institutions.

## The Two Different Illinois Thresholds

Illinois has two applicable thresholds, and confusing them is the most common mistake people make:

### Statewide Threshold: $150,000

The standard Illinois Small Estate Affidavit can be used for estates with **total gross assets of $150,000 or less** (as amended August 2025). This applies to residents and property in all Illinois counties **except** Cook County, where the formula is different.

### Cook County: $20,000 Base + $10,000 Per Surviving Minor Child

> **Cook County is different.** If the deceased lived in Cook County or the property is located in Cook County, the applicable limit is not $150,000.

Cook County's small estate threshold is calculated as:
- **$20,000 base**, plus
- **$10,000 for each surviving minor child** of the deceased

**Examples:**
- No minor children: $20,000 maximum
- One minor child: $30,000 maximum
- Three minor children: $50,000 maximum

This formula means a Cook County family with three young children could potentially use the affidavit for an estate worth up to $50,000 — but a Cook County family with no minor children is limited to $20,000.

For most Cook County estates above $20,000 (with no minor children), formal probate or another transfer mechanism will be necessary.

## Who Can Use an Illinois Small Estate Affidavit?

Under 755 ILCS 5/25-1, the following people may present a Small Estate Affidavit:

- An heir of the deceased (as defined by Illinois intestate succession law)
- A legatee (someone named in a will)
- A creditor of the estate (to collect amounts owed)

Additional requirements:
- [ ] The total assets of the estate (excluding non-probate assets like life insurance with beneficiaries) fall within the applicable threshold
- [ ] At least **30 days** have passed since the date of death
- [ ] No probate proceedings are pending or have been filed
- [ ] The affidavit is signed under penalty of perjury

The 30-day waiting period is a firm requirement. It applies regardless of the urgency of the heir's financial situation.

## What Assets Qualify?

The Illinois Small Estate Affidavit typically covers:

**Yes — included in the $150,000 cap:**
- Bank and savings accounts (without payable-on-death designations)
- Checking accounts
- Certificates of deposit
- Personal property, household goods, jewelry
- Vehicles (though the Illinois Secretary of State has its own process for titled vehicles)

**No — these generally pass outside the affidavit:**
- Life insurance with named beneficiaries
- Retirement accounts (IRA, 401(k)) with named beneficiaries
- Payable-on-death (POD) bank accounts
- Joint tenancy property (passes automatically to the surviving joint tenant)
- Real estate in Illinois (real property generally requires probate or a transfer on death instrument)

**Important distinction on real property:** Unlike Arizona, Illinois's Small Estate Affidavit does not typically cover real estate transfers. Real property in Illinois generally requires probate or another instrument (such as an Illinois Residential Real Property Transfer on Death Instrument). If the estate's main asset is a home, the Small Estate Affidavit alone will likely not be sufficient.

## Step-by-Step: Filing an Illinois Small Estate Affidavit

### Step 1: Confirm Your Eligibility

- Verify that 30 days have passed from the date of death
- Calculate the total probate estate value (exclude life insurance with beneficiaries, POD accounts, etc.)
- Confirm you are in the correct county — Cook County has different limits
- Confirm no probate proceeding is open

### Step 2: Obtain the Correct Form

Illinois does not have a single mandatory statewide form, but 755 ILCS 5/25-1 specifies what the affidavit must contain. The form must include:

- Deceased's name, date of death, and last address
- Names and addresses of all known heirs
- Total value of the estate's assets
- A statement that no probate proceeding is pending
- A statement that 30 days have elapsed
- A description of the assets being claimed
- The affiant's relationship to the deceased
- Signature under penalty of perjury

### Step 3: Have It Notarized

The affidavit must be signed and notarized. Unlike some states, Illinois also often requires the affidavit to be signed under oath or affirmation, reinforcing the penalty-of-perjury language.

### Step 4: Present to the Asset Holder

Present the notarized affidavit to the bank, brokerage, or other institution holding the assets, along with:

- Death certificate
- Your government-issued photo ID
- Any additional documentation the institution requires

The institution is then permitted — and legally authorized — to transfer the assets to you.

## What Banks Actually Do with the Affidavit

Banks are sometimes the biggest obstacle. Here is what typically happens:

**Smaller community banks and credit unions:** Generally more cooperative. They often accept a properly prepared affidavit with a death certificate and will release funds promptly.

**Large national banks (Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo):** May have their own internal affidavit forms and may require a branch manager's approval for amounts above $5,000–$10,000. They are legally authorized to honor the affidavit, but individual employees may be unfamiliar with the law.

## Cook County: Practical Considerations

If you are dealing with a Cook County estate:

1. **Calculate the threshold accurately.** Count surviving minor children carefully. "Minor" means under 18 in Illinois.

2. **If the estate exceeds the Cook County limit:** You have a few options — formal probate, a joint tenancy if applicable, or (for real estate) an Illinois Transfer on Death Instrument going forward. An Illinois probate attorney can advise on the best path.

3. **Use county-specific forms.** The Cook County Circuit Court Probate Division occasionally publishes its own forms and guidance. Check the Cook County court website for current resources.

4. **Cook County Recorder of Deeds:** Real property in Cook County is recorded with the Cook County Recorder of Deeds (not the circuit court). If the estate has real property, the affidavit process alone is generally not sufficient.

## Common Mistakes

**Using the wrong threshold.** Applying the $150,000 statewide limit to a Cook County estate when the actual limit is far lower is a common and costly error.

**Counting non-probate assets.** Life insurance proceeds with named beneficiaries, POD accounts, and 401(k)s do not count toward the $150,000 threshold. Families often assume the estate is over the limit when it is not.

**Not waiting 30 days.** An affidavit presented before the 30-day period is legally defective.

**Assuming real estate is covered.** Unlike Arizona, Illinois's Small Estate Affidavit generally does not cover real property transfers.

**Using pre-2025 forms.** The August 2025 amendment changed both the threshold and certain procedural elements. A form from before that date may be outdated and should not be used.

## When This Document May Not Help You

This document may not help you if:

**The estate includes real property.** Illinois's Small Estate Affidavit does not cover real estate transfers. A home, rental property, or land requires formal probate or an Illinois Transfer on Death Instrument (which must be set up before death). If the primary estate asset is a house, the Small Estate Affidavit alone will not resolve it.

**You are in Cook County and the estate exceeds the formula limit.** Cook County's threshold is substantially lower than the statewide $150,000. A Cook County family with no minor children is limited to $20,000. Many families discover this mismatch only after preparing documents based on the statewide limit.

**A probate proceeding is already open.** If anyone has filed a probate petition in any Illinois circuit court for this estate, the Small Estate Affidavit process is no longer available. Check the circuit court's records before proceeding.

**The affiant does not qualify.** Illinois 755 ILCS 5/25-1 limits who can present the affidavit to heirs, legatees, and creditors. A friend, caregiver, or neighbor who is not an heir under Illinois law cannot use the affidavit even if they were close to the deceased.

## What Banks Commonly Reject — and What to Do About It

Illinois bank rejections follow predictable patterns. Here is what actually causes them and how to handle each:

**Using an outdated pre-2025 form.** A form that reflects the old, pre-August 2025 threshold may cause a bank compliance officer to flag the document as potentially defective. Use only forms reflecting the current 755 ILCS 5/25-1 amendment threshold of $150,000.

**Missing the penalty-of-perjury statement.** Illinois requires the affidavit to be signed under oath with explicit penalty-of-perjury language. A form that omits this — or where the language is informal — is legally defective. Banks that review it carefully may reject it on this basis.

**Applying the statewide threshold in Cook County.** If a bank officer or their compliance team realizes the estate is in Cook County but the affidavit asserts the $150,000 statewide threshold applies, they may refuse the document. Cook County's formula is different and must be applied correctly.

**No death certificate presented.** Always bring a certified death certificate. Banks treat it as mandatory supporting documentation regardless of what the statute technically requires.

**Account has a POD designation.** If the account already has a payable-on-death designation, the Small Estate Affidavit does not apply. The POD beneficiary has direct rights to the funds and claims them separately using the death certificate and their own ID.

**Branch employee unfamiliar with Illinois small estate law.** The **bank instruction letter** included with every smallestateform.com document cites 755 ILCS 5/25-1 specifically and explains the bank's legal authority to release funds. Ask for a branch manager if the first employee hesitates.

## Mistakes Institutions See Most Often in Illinois Small Estate Filings

**Applying the $150,000 statewide limit in Cook County.** This is the most consequential and most common error. The Cook County formula ($20,000 base + $10,000 per surviving minor child) applies to any estate where the deceased lived in Cook County or where the assets are located in Cook County. Using the wrong threshold creates legal exposure for the affiant who signs under penalty of perjury.

**Counting POD accounts and retirement funds toward the threshold.** Life insurance with a named beneficiary, 401(k)s, IRAs, and payable-on-death bank accounts are not part of the probate estate and do not count toward the $150,000 threshold. Families often overestimate the estate value and conclude they must go through probate when they do not.

**Not waiting the full 30 days.** Even if a bank accepts the affidavit before the 30-day period has elapsed, the affiant's sworn statement that 30 days have passed is technically false. Wait the full period — and document the date of death clearly on the form.

**Using the affidavit for real estate.** Illinois's Small Estate Affidavit covers personal property only. Attempting to use it to transfer a house will be rejected by title companies and county recorders, and the error may require an attorney to untangle.

**Using a pre-2025 form from the Secretary of State's website.** The Secretary of State PDF that still ranks at the top of search results reflects pre-2025 law with the old, lower threshold. Do not use it. A form reflecting outdated law cites incorrect statutory language and may be treated as defective by a bank compliance team.

**Signing before the notary is present.** The affidavit must be signed in the notary's presence. A pre-signed form is technically defective under Illinois law, even if the notary acknowledges it after the fact.
