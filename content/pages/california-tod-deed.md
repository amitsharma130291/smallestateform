## What a California TOD Deed Is — and Why Searches for It Are Surging

A Transfer on Death Deed (sometimes called a beneficiary deed) lets a California homeowner name who will receive their property when they die — without a will, without a trust, and without probate. The property transfers automatically upon death, recorded at the county recorder's office.

Search interest in California TOD Deeds increased roughly 900% year-over-year after AB 1052 made California one of the first large states to formally authorize revocable TOD Deeds for real property in 2022. Before that legislation, California homeowners who wanted to avoid probate had limited options: a living trust (which typically costs $2,000–$5,000 to create) or joint tenancy (which carries its own complications, including gift tax exposure and loss of control).

The TOD Deed is not for everyone — but for a homeowner who simply wants to ensure their house goes to a specific person at death without court involvement, it may be the simplest and least expensive tool available.

---

## How It Differs from a Will and a Trust

Most people discover TOD Deeds while researching probate avoidance. Here is how the tools compare:

| Feature | TOD Deed | Will | Revocable Living Trust |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avoids probate | ✅ Yes | ❌ No | ✅ Yes |
| Attorney required | ❌ Not required | Recommended | Strongly recommended |
| Revocable | ✅ Anytime before death | ✅ Anytime | ✅ Anytime |
| Cost to set up | ~$15–$21 county recording fee | $1,000–$3,000+ (attorney) | $2,000–$5,000+ (attorney) |
| Covers all assets | ❌ Real property only | ✅ All assets | ✅ All assets |
| Takes effect | At death | After probate | At death |
| Controls management during lifetime | Completely — no change | Completely — no change | Partially (trustee rules apply) |

The key trade-off: a TOD Deed covers **real property only**. If the estate has significant other assets — bank accounts, vehicles, investments — those will still require separate planning (POD designations, beneficiary designations, or a trust). For a homeowner whose main estate asset is their house, a TOD Deed plus beneficiary designations on financial accounts can largely eliminate the need for probate.

## Who Should Consider a California TOD Deed

The TOD Deed is particularly well-suited for:

**Homeowners who haven't set up a trust** and want simple probate avoidance without paying $3,000–$5,000 for trust drafting. A TOD Deed accomplishes the same result for real property at a fraction of the cost.

**People with a clear, straightforward beneficiary** — one adult child, one domestic partner. Multiple beneficiaries create questions (what if one predeceases the grantor?) that the deed can address but that should be thought through carefully.

**Owners of rental property** who want the property to pass to heirs without court involvement or the disruption a long probate process would cause to tenants and rental income.

**People not ready for full trust planning** — the TOD Deed can serve as an interim measure while more comprehensive planning is arranged.

The TOD Deed may be a poorer fit for:

- Estates with a mortgage subject to a due-on-sale clause (the lender cannot accelerate on transfer at death, but the heir should be prepared to qualify for the loan or refinance)
- Properties with environmental issues or complex title problems
- Situations where the grantor anticipates needing Medi-Cal benefits (the TOD Deed may interact with Medi-Cal recovery — consult an elder law attorney)
- Situations with multiple beneficiaries of differing ages where management and eventual sale could be contentious

## Key Features of the California TOD Deed

**No asset threshold.** The California TOD Deed can be used for any California real property, regardless of value. A $5 million Palo Alto home and a $200,000 Fresno rental both qualify.

**Revocable until death.** You retain complete ownership and control of the property during your lifetime. You can sell it, mortgage it, lease it, or live in it. The beneficiary has no rights until you die. You can revoke the TOD Deed at any time by recording a revocation deed.

**Multiple beneficiaries allowed.** You may name more than one beneficiary. They take the property as tenants in common (each with an equal undivided share) unless you specify percentages. You can also name alternate beneficiaries who take if a primary beneficiary predeceases you.

**The grantor must be competent.** The TOD Deed requires the grantor to have legal capacity at the time of signing. A TOD Deed signed by someone who lacked capacity can be challenged.

## How to Complete a California TOD Deed

### What You Need Before You Start

- [ ] Your full legal name as it appears on the current deed
- [ ] The full legal name of your beneficiary (and any alternate beneficiaries)
- [ ] The **Assessor's Parcel Number (APN)** for the property
- [ ] The legal description of the property (from the current deed, not just the address)
- [ ] The county where the property is located

### What Is an APN?

The Assessor's Parcel Number is a unique identifier assigned to your property by the county tax assessor. It is typically shown on your property tax bill, and you can also look it up on the county assessor's website using your address. The APN ensures the TOD Deed is recorded against the correct property.

### Completing the Form

The California TOD Deed (officially called a "Revocable Transfer on Death Deed" in Civil Code §5642) must include:

1. Grantor's full legal name and mailing address
2. Beneficiary's full legal name (and alternate beneficiary, if any)
3. Property's legal description — not just the street address
4. The APN
5. A statement that the transfer is revocable and takes effect only at the grantor's death

### Signing and Notarization

The grantor must sign the TOD Deed before a notary public. The beneficiary does not need to sign and does not need to be notified during the grantor's lifetime.

### Recording with the County Recorder

The TOD Deed must be **recorded with the county recorder** in the county where the property is located. Recording must occur within 60 days of notarization (Civil Code §5628). An unrecorded TOD Deed is ineffective — the transfer will not occur at death if the deed was never recorded.

#### California County Recorder Fees (2026)

| County | Fee Per Page |
|---|---|
| Los Angeles County | $21 |
| San Francisco County | $15 |
| San Diego County | $21 |
| Orange County | $21 |
| Riverside County | $21 |

Most TOD Deeds are 2–3 pages. Add the per-page fee accordingly. Some counties add a small additional charge for the preliminary change of ownership form or documentary transfer tax (though no documentary transfer tax is due on a TOD Deed during the grantor's lifetime — this applies at the beneficiary's end upon death).

## What Happens When You Die?

The beneficiary does not automatically receive the property the moment you die. They must take certain steps:

1. **Wait 120 days** (or until any will has been admitted to probate, whichever is later) — this is a waiting period designed to protect creditors and allow will contests to be filed
2. **File an affidavit of survivorship** with the county recorder, along with a certified death certificate
3. The property is then recorded in the beneficiary's name

During the 120-day waiting period, creditors of the estate may make claims against the property. This is different from a living trust, where there is typically no similar waiting period for creditors after death.

---

## How to Revoke a California TOD Deed

Revocation is straightforward during the grantor's lifetime. Simply:

1. **Complete a revocation deed** — a one-page document that identifies the property and states that the TOD Deed is revoked
2. **Sign before a notary public**
3. **Record with the county recorder**

The revocation is effective only upon recording. Telling your beneficiary you changed your mind, crossing out the TOD Deed, or signing an unrecorded revocation has no legal effect. The property will still transfer to the named beneficiary at death if the recorded TOD Deed is not revoked.

A new TOD Deed naming a different beneficiary does not automatically revoke the prior one — it is safest to record an explicit revocation and then record the new deed.

## Common Questions

**What if the beneficiary dies before I do?**
If the named beneficiary predeceases you and you have not named an alternate beneficiary, the TOD Deed fails and the property passes according to your will (or, if no will, by intestate succession). Name an alternate beneficiary to avoid this.

**Can I have multiple beneficiaries?**
Yes. They take as tenants in common, each with an equal undivided share unless you specify percentages (e.g., 60% to one child, 40% to another). Co-owners must agree on management decisions after the grantor's death, which can create conflict.

**Does a TOD Deed override my will?**
Generally, yes — for that specific property. The TOD Deed controls the disposition of the real property it covers regardless of what your will says about it. If you want a different distribution after you sign a TOD Deed, you must revoke it or execute a new one.

**What if I have a mortgage?**
The federal Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act protects against lender acceleration when property is transferred at death to an heir. Your lender generally cannot call the loan due simply because you died and the property transferred under the TOD Deed. However, the beneficiary should be prepared to either assume the mortgage (lender consent typically required) or refinance.

**Can I change the beneficiary?**
Yes, at any time before death. Record a revocation deed or a new TOD Deed naming a different beneficiary.

**What if I have a homestead exemption?**
Recording a TOD Deed does not affect your homestead exemption during your lifetime. The exemption continues until you die.

## Should You Use a California TOD Deed, a Living Trust, or a Will?

**The TOD Deed is the simplest and least expensive option for homeowners who want to avoid probate for their real property.** It covers real property only — if you have other significant assets, you may need additional planning alongside it.

| Your situation | Best approach |
|---|---|
| You own California real property and want it to pass to one or two clear beneficiaries at death, no probate | TOD Deed — simple, inexpensive, revocable |
| You want to cover all assets (bank accounts, investments, personal property) in one plan | Revocable living trust — more comprehensive but costs $2,000–$5,000 |
| You have minor children as beneficiaries | Consult an attorney — minor beneficiaries cannot hold property directly; a trust is usually better |
| You are receiving or anticipate applying for Medi-Cal benefits | Consult an elder law attorney — TOD Deeds may interact with Medi-Cal recovery |
| You have multiple co-owners with complex shares | Revocable living trust — cleaner management and administration |
| You have already set up a living trust | Transfer property into the trust rather than using a TOD Deed |

## When This Document May Not Help You

This document may not help you if:

**Your primary concern is assets other than real property.** The California TOD Deed covers real property only — the house or land itself. Bank accounts, investment accounts, vehicles, and personal property are not covered. For those assets, use payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designations directly with the financial institution, or establish a trust.

**You anticipate needing Medi-Cal long-term care benefits.** California's Medi-Cal estate recovery program can make claims against property that passes to a beneficiary through a TOD Deed. If you are 55 or older and may need Medi-Cal for long-term care, an elder law attorney should review the implications before you record a TOD Deed.

**Your named beneficiary is a minor.** Minors cannot legally hold real property in California. If a minor beneficiary inherits under a TOD Deed, a conservatorship or guardian of the estate may be required — exactly the kind of court involvement the TOD Deed is designed to avoid. If you want to leave property to a minor, a trust with a trustee is the better tool.

**The property has significant environmental contamination or title defects.** These issues require legal resolution regardless of how the property transfers. A TOD Deed does not fix underlying title problems — the beneficiary inherits them.

## What County Recorders Commonly Send Back — and What to Do About It

California county recorder rejections are usually technical, not substantive. Here are the most common:

**Missing Assessor's Parcel Number (APN).** Most county recorders require the APN on the first page of any recorded document. An APN-less TOD Deed may be rejected on submission. The APN is on your property tax bill or available through the county assessor's online lookup tool.

**Legal description is incomplete or uses a street address only.** The county recorder requires the full legal description exactly as it appears in the existing deed — lot, block, subdivision, section, township, and range, or metes-and-bounds. "123 Main Street, Los Angeles" is not a legal description.

**Document recorded after the 60-day window.** Under Civil Code §5628, the TOD Deed is only effective if recorded within 60 days of notarization. A document submitted on day 62 will be recorded but will be legally defective. If you miss the window, execute and sign a new TOD Deed and start the 60-day clock fresh.

**Missing preliminary change of ownership report.** Many California counties require a Preliminary Change of Ownership Report (PCOR) to accompany recorded deeds. A TOD Deed may be rejected without it — check your county recorder's current requirements before filing.

**Grantor's name does not match the vesting on the existing deed.** The TOD Deed grantor must match the name on the recorded title exactly. A name discrepancy (full name vs. nickname, name change after marriage) will cause rejection. Use the name exactly as it appears on the current deed.

## Mistakes People Make With California TOD Deeds

**Not recording within 60 days.** This is the most consequential error — and it happens more often than you might expect. Families sign and notarize the deed and then set it aside intending to file it later. If more than 60 days pass, the deed is legally ineffective and must be redone.

**Assuming the beneficiary receives property immediately at death.** The California TOD Deed process requires a 120-day waiting period after death before the beneficiary can complete the transfer. During this window, creditors can make claims. The property does not instantly transfer the moment the grantor dies.

**Naming only a primary beneficiary without an alternate.** If the named beneficiary predeceases the grantor and no alternate is listed, the TOD Deed fails entirely. The property then passes through the will (if one exists) or by intestate succession — potentially through probate. Always name at least one alternate beneficiary.

**Recording a new TOD Deed without revoking the old one.** A newer TOD Deed does not automatically revoke an earlier one in California. Recording a second deed creates ambiguity. Always record an explicit revocation deed before (or simultaneously with) recording a new TOD Deed naming a different beneficiary.

**Believing the TOD Deed eliminates the mortgage.** The beneficiary inherits the property subject to any existing mortgage. Under the federal Garn-St. Germain Act, the lender cannot call the loan due solely because of the transfer at death — but the beneficiary must either assume the loan or refinance it. The property comes with the debt.
