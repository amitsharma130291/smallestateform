## What Is a Small Estate Affidavit?

A small estate affidavit is a sworn legal document that allows an heir to collect a deceased person's assets without opening a formal probate estate. The heir signs the document, has it notarized, and presents it to whoever holds the assets — a bank, a brokerage, the DMV, or a county recorder — rather than going through a court proceeding.

Most states have created this process specifically because formal probate is expensive and slow relative to the size of many modest estates. An estate worth $80,000 does not need $10,000 in probate attorney fees and 12 months of waiting before the family can access it.

The affidavit works because the heir swears under oath to certain facts:
- The deceased has died
- There is no pending probate proceeding
- The required waiting period has elapsed
- The estate falls within the applicable threshold
- The affiant is a legitimate heir

The asset holder then has legal authority — and in many states, legal obligation — to release the assets based on that sworn statement.

## How a Small Estate Affidavit Works in Practice

The process, simplified:

1. **A person dies** — with or without a will
2. **The family identifies assets** — bank accounts, a car, personal property, sometimes real estate
3. **They calculate the estate value** to determine if it's under their state's threshold
4. **They wait the required period** (30–45 days in most states)
5. **They complete and notarize the affidavit**
6. **They present it** to the bank, DMV, or county recorder
7. **The assets transfer** without a judge, without a lawyer, without months of waiting

The entire process can take as little as a few weeks from death to asset access in straightforward cases. Formal probate in the same state might take 9–18 months.

## Small Estate Affidavit vs. Full Probate: When to Use Each

| Factor | Small Estate Affidavit | Full Probate |
|---|---|---|
| Estate value | Below state threshold | Any size |
| Court involvement | None | Required |
| Attorney needed | Usually not | Strongly recommended |
| Cost | Filing fees ($0–$50+) | $3,000–$30,000+ |
| Time | Weeks | 9–18+ months |
| Best for | Clear heirs, straightforward assets | Contested estates, large debts, complex assets |
| Creditor protection | Limited | Formal bar on late creditor claims |
| Works for real property | Depends on state | Yes |

**When to choose probate instead:**
- The estate significantly exceeds the state threshold
- There are disputed heirs or a contested will
- The estate has significant unpaid debts or creditor claims
- Real estate is involved and your state's small estate affidavit doesn't cover real property
- The family cannot agree on who the heirs are

### California Small Estate and Transfer on Death Deed

California's threshold for personal property stands at $239,700 for deaths after April 1, 2026 — adjusted periodically by the Judicial Council for inflation. For real property, California does not have a small estate affidavit process; instead, homeowners should consider a **Transfer on Death Deed** (Civil Code §5600–5696), which transfers property automatically at death without probate.

California TOD Deeds are revocable any time before death, can name multiple beneficiaries, and cost only the county recorder's filing fee ($15–$21/page). They became available statewide under AB 1052 in 2022 and have seen massive growth in searches since.

### Arizona Small Estate Affidavit (HB2116, March 2026)

Arizona raised its limits dramatically in March 2026 under HB2116: personal property to $200,000 and real property to $300,000 — some of the highest limits in the country. The 30-day waiting period applies statewide. Maricopa, Pima, and Yavapai counties use county-specific forms.

### Illinois Small Estate Affidavit (Amended August 2025)

Illinois raised its statewide threshold to $150,000 in August 2025. Cook County is different: the limit is $20,000 plus $10,000 per surviving minor child. Illinois's small estate affidavit covers personal property only — real estate generally requires probate or an Illinois Transfer on Death Instrument.

### Texas Affidavit of Heirship

Texas handles property transfers differently from most states. For personal property under $75,000, Texas has a Small Estate Affidavit (Texas Estates Code §§ 205.001–205.009). For real property, the primary tool is the **Affidavit of Heirship** (Texas Estates Code Chapter 203), which has **no asset threshold** — it can be used for a ranch worth millions or a lot worth $50,000.

The Affidavit of Heirship requires two disinterested witnesses and must be recorded with the county clerk to be effective against third parties.

| Creditor claims | Heirs remain personally exposed | Formal bar after creditor window closes |
| Contested heirs | Not suitable — affidavit won't resolve disputes | Appropriate — judge decides |
| Out-of-state property | Must follow that state's rules separately | Same |
| Title insurance afterward | Generally accepted (AZ, TX after 5 years) | Accepted immediately |

### When Probate Is Unavoidable

Four situations where a Small Estate Affidavit will not work and formal probate is the required path:

**1. The estate exceeds the state threshold.** Most states calculate the threshold against gross value, not equity. A home appraised at $350,000 does not qualify for Arizona's $300,000 real property limit regardless of the outstanding mortgage. When gross value exceeds the threshold, the Superior Court is the only option.

**2. Heirs are in dispute.** A Small Estate Affidavit is a sworn statement, not a judicial ruling. If family members disagree on who the heirs are, whether a will is valid, or whether an heir was already paid their share, no affidavit can resolve that — only a judge can. Presenting an affidavit in a contested situation creates legal exposure for the person who signs it.

**3. The estate has substantial unpaid debts or creditor claims.** Small estate affidavits do not create a formal creditor-claims process. A creditor whose claim is ignored can still pursue the heirs who received assets through an affidavit. Probate, by contrast, creates a formal window during which creditors must file — and bars late claims afterward. Significant debt makes probate's creditor process worth the cost.

**4. A probate case has already been filed.** Once anyone petitions any state court to open a probate estate for a deceased person, the small estate affidavit process becomes unavailable in that state. This is true even if the petitioner later abandons the case — the estate is considered "in probate" until the court formally closes it.

## How to Determine If You Qualify

Work through this framework:

**Step 1: Has the waiting period elapsed?**
Most states require 30 days. Some require 40 days. A few require as little as 10 days (Colorado). Check your state's requirement.

**Step 2: What are the assets?**
List all assets in the estate. Exclude:
- Life insurance with named beneficiaries (passes directly to beneficiary)
- Retirement accounts with named beneficiaries (same)
- Jointly-held property (passes to the surviving joint tenant)
- Payable-on-death bank accounts (passes directly)

What remains is the "probate estate" — the assets the small estate affidavit process addresses.

**Step 3: What is the total value?**
Add up the probate estate. If it's under your state's threshold, you likely qualify.

**Step 4: What type of property is involved?**
Some states cover personal property only (bank accounts, vehicles, household goods). Others also cover real property. Check whether your state's affidavit covers the specific type of asset you need to access.

**Step 5: Is probate already open?**
If someone has already filed a probate petition for this estate, the small estate affidavit process is generally not available.

**Step 6: Are there disputed heirs?**
If family members disagree on who the heirs are, or there is a contested will, the small estate affidavit process may not be appropriate — a court needs to resolve those disputes first.

## What "Gross Value" vs. "Net Value" Means for Thresholds

Many states calculate the threshold based on **gross value** — the total value of assets without subtracting debts or mortgages. A house worth $250,000 with a $200,000 mortgage is counted as $250,000 for threshold purposes in most states.

This is a trap that catches many families. Example: Arizona's real property threshold is $300,000 gross. A home worth $280,000 with a $200,000 mortgage qualifies (the threshold is met by gross value). But the heir who receives the property also receives it subject to that $200,000 mortgage — there is no magic debt elimination.

Always check whether your state uses gross or net value, and understand that qualifying for the affidavit process does not eliminate the estate's debts.

## The Bank Instruction Letter: Why It Matters

The single most common complaint from families using small estate affidavits is that the bank refuses to cooperate. A branch employee who has never seen the form before may say "we need a court order" — even when the law does not require one.

The most effective remedy is a **bank instruction letter**: a document that cites the specific statute, explains the bank's legal authority to release the funds, and notes their potential liability for refusing a legally sufficient affidavit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a small estate affidavit and how does it work?**
A small estate affidavit is a sworn legal document that allows heirs to collect a deceased person's assets without probate, when the estate's value falls below a state-specific threshold. The heir signs the document before a notary and presents it to the asset holder (bank, DMV, etc.), who is authorized to release the assets without a court order.

**What happens if the estate is over the threshold?**
Formal probate is generally required. In some states, there are intermediate options — California has a simplified probate procedure for estates under $500,000; some states have summary administration. Consult an attorney if the estate exceeds the small estate threshold.

**Does a small estate affidavit work for bank accounts?**
Yes, in most states — for accounts that do not already have a payable-on-death (POD) designation. Present the affidavit with a death certificate and photo ID. A bank instruction letter citing the applicable statute significantly improves cooperation.

**Does a small estate affidavit work for real estate?**
It depends on the state. Arizona and Nevada allow it for real property. Texas uses an Affidavit of Heirship for real property (no threshold). Illinois does not cover real property. California does not cover real property through this mechanism (use a TOD Deed instead). Check your state's specific rules.

**How long does a small estate affidavit take?**
The waiting period (usually 30 days) is typically the longest step. After that, completing the form takes 1–2 hours. Recording or presenting it takes a single visit or mail filing. Total time from death to asset access: as little as 5–6 weeks in straightforward cases.

**Do I need a lawyer for a small estate affidavit?**
No. The process is specifically designed to be used without attorney involvement. However, if the estate is complicated — significant debts, disputed heirs, real estate in a state that requires probate — consulting an attorney is the right call.

**Can multiple heirs use one small estate affidavit?**
Yes, typically. All heirs can be listed on a single affidavit. All may need to sign it, depending on state law. If heirs disagree, the process becomes much more complicated.

**What is the difference between a small estate affidavit and an affidavit of heirship?**
A small estate affidavit is used to collect specific assets when the estate is below a threshold — it generally requires a waiting period and is presented to asset holders. An affidavit of heirship (used primarily in Texas and some other states) establishes the identity of heirs for property succession purposes and is typically recorded with a county recorder. The two are different tools for different purposes, though they overlap in some states.
